The standard retirement planning advice was not designed for women's financial realities.

It was designed around a career that runs uninterrupted from 22 to 65, with consistent income growth and no career gaps. That is not most women's experience. The gender pay gap, career interruptions for caregiving, longer life expectancy, and the lingering effects of financial dependence on partners all mean that women who follow standard retirement advice are often significantly underprepared.

This is what a retirement plan that actually accounts for your reality looks like.

## Why Women Need More Retirement Savings Than Men

Women live longer. On average, women outlive men by five to seven years. That means a longer retirement to fund, more years of healthcare costs, and more exposure to inflation eroding purchasing power over time.

Women are more likely to take career breaks. For childcare, for elder care, for a partner's career relocation, for personal health. Each break interrupts contributions to retirement accounts and Social Security credits while creating a gap in compound growth that is difficult to fully recover.

Women earn less on average across their careers due to the gender pay gap, which results in lower Social Security benefits and smaller retirement account balances from percentage-based contributions.

These are structural realities, not personal failures. But they require a structural response in how you approach retirement planning.

## Start With Your Retirement Number

Calculate how much you actually need.

A commonly used guideline is that you need twenty-five times your annual expenses to retire comfortably. If you expect to spend fifty thousand dollars per year in retirement, you need approximately one point two five million dollars saved.

Add to this the extra years women statistically need to fund. If you are planning for a 30-year retirement rather than 20, your number is larger. Add healthcare cost inflation, which consistently exceeds general inflation. Add the possibility that your Social Security benefit may be lower than a man's in a comparable career.

The number may feel large. It is supposed to motivate early action, not produce despair. Even small amounts invested early compound significantly over decades.

## Maximize Tax-Advantaged Accounts First

Before any other retirement saving, take full advantage of every tax-advantaged account available to you.

Your employer's 401k, at minimum up to the employer match. Never leave matching contributions unclaimed. That is an immediate one hundred percent return on the matched portion.

A Roth IRA if you are income-eligible. Contributions are made after tax, growth is tax-free, and withdrawals in retirement are completely tax-free. For women who expect to be in a higher tax bracket in retirement than currently, the Roth is particularly valuable.

A Health Savings Account if you have a high-deductible health plan. The HSA is the only account in existence that is triple tax-advantaged. Contributions are tax-deductible, growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free. Healthcare is typically the largest retirement expense for women. Funding an HSA specifically for future healthcare costs is one of the smartest retirement moves available.

## Plan for Social Security as Partial Income Only

Social Security was not designed to fund a comfortable retirement alone. It was designed as a supplement to personal savings and pensions.

For women, Social Security benefits are typically lower than for men due to lower average lifetime earnings. If you have career gaps, your benefit may be lower still since it is calculated based on your 35 highest-earning years.

Know your estimated Social Security benefit by creating an account at ssa.gov and reviewing your earnings history and projected benefit. Plan your personal savings to fill the gap between that benefit and the retirement income you need.

If you are married, understand how spousal benefits and survivor benefits work. If your spouse has a significantly higher Social Security benefit, coordinating when each of you claims can maximize lifetime income significantly.

## Do Not Rely on a Partner's Retirement Plan as Your Own

This is the most important message in this entire article.

Women who plan to retire comfortably on a partner's savings and Social Security are exposed to risks that have nothing to do with poor personal financial management.

Divorce financially devastating to both partners, particularly the one who left the workforce. Death of a partner, especially early. Disability. An unexpected divergence in retirement vision. All of these can upend a retirement plan built entirely on a partner's financial foundation.

This is not cynicism about relationships. It is financial realism. Your own retirement accounts, in your own name, contributing consistently regardless of your relationship status, are non-negotiable.

## Consider Sequence of Returns Risk

This is the danger of experiencing poor investment returns early in retirement, which can permanently deplete a portfolio even if average long-term returns are fine.

Withdrawing from a portfolio that has just dropped thirty percent during a market downturn sells shares at a loss and reduces the number of shares available to recover when markets improve. This sequence of poor early returns followed by good later ones produces much worse outcomes than the reverse.

Women, who statistically have longer retirements, face more exposure to sequence of returns risk. Holding one to two years of living expenses in cash or stable assets when entering retirement provides a buffer that allows you to avoid selling investments during downturns.

## Review Your Retirement Plan Annually

Your retirement savings target, your current balances, your projected Social Security benefit, and your expected retirement expenses should all be reviewed every year.

Life changes. Income changes. Expenses change. Retirement vision changes. An annual review keeps your plan aligned with your current reality and allows early course correction when adjustments are needed.

The women who retire securely are not those who figured it all out perfectly early. They are the ones who started, stayed engaged, and adjusted consistently over decades.

You have time to build this. Start where you are.
