Getting out of debt is not complicated. It is just hard.

There is a difference. Complicated means you do not know what to do. Hard means you know what to do and doing it consistently over a long period of time requires more energy than one burst of motivation can provide.

This plan is realistic because it acknowledges the hard part. It does not promise a magic system. It gives you a clear sequence of steps and an honest picture of what the process actually involves.

## Step One: Stop the Bleeding

Before you can pay down debt, you need to stop adding to it.

This does not mean you can never use credit again. It means identifying why new debt is accumulating and addressing the specific cause.

If you are using credit cards to cover regular monthly expenses, your income does not cover your cost of living and the gap needs to close either through expense reduction or income increase. Using debt to paper over a structural budget deficit means you will still be in debt in five years regardless of how hard you work on payoff.

If you are using credit for discretionary spending beyond your means, the budget needs specific categories and limits that replace the card as the spending signal.

Freeze your credit cards physically if the temptation to use them is strong. Put them in a bag of water in the freezer. The ridiculous barrier it creates gives you a pause that prevents impulse use.

## Step Two: Know Every Debt You Owe

Write every debt on a single page. The creditor name, current balance, minimum monthly payment, and interest rate.

Every debt. Credit cards, student loans, car loans, personal loans, medical debt, money owed to family. All of it visible in one place.

This is the most avoided step in debt payoff because seeing the full total is confronting. It is also the most essential. You cannot build a plan around numbers you are not willing to look at.

## Step Three: Build a Minimal Emergency Fund First

This sounds counterintuitive when you are trying to get out of debt. Save first?

Yes. A small emergency fund of one thousand dollars prevents debt relapse. Without it, the first unexpected expense sends you straight back to the credit card. With it, emergencies are absorbed without adding to the balance you are trying to reduce.

One thousand dollars. In a separate savings account. Before you attack debt aggressively. This is not optional.

## Step Four: Choose Your Payoff Method

Two proven methods exist. Choose one and commit to it.

The debt snowball lists your debts from smallest balance to largest. You make minimum payments on everything and throw every available extra dollar at the smallest balance. When it is gone, that payment rolls to the next smallest. You work through them in order until all are paid.

The psychological wins from eliminating debts completely fuel motivation that keeps the process going. Most financial counselors recommend this method for most people because human motivation matters as much as math.

The debt avalanche lists debts from highest interest rate to lowest. Extra money goes to the highest interest debt first, regardless of balance. This method saves the most money in interest paid over time.

If you are motivated by math and can stay focused without the quick wins of eliminating small balances, the avalanche saves you more money. If you need to see accounts closed to stay motivated, the snowball is more likely to take you all the way.

## Step Five: Find Your Extra Money

Minimum payments keep debt from growing. Extra payments eliminate it.

Even fifty extra dollars per month on your target debt accelerates payoff significantly. Finding that fifty dollars is the practical work of this step.

Review your budget category by category. Which one has room? What subscription can you cancel this month? What meal can you cook instead of ordering? What item around your house could you sell this week?

The amount matters less than the consistency. Fifty extra dollars every month for a year is six hundred dollars applied to debt in addition to minimums. Over two or three years, it meaningfully changes your payoff timeline.

## Step Six: Increase Your Income If the Math Requires It

There is a limit to how much you can cut. If your expenses are already minimal and you have no flexibility left in the budget, the only lever left is income.

A side hustle generating two hundred extra dollars per month applies to your debt payoff and accelerates the timeline considerably. It does not need to be permanent. It needs to last long enough to close the gap between your current debt and zero.

Temporary income increases for a defined purpose are sustainable in a way that open-ended financial pressure is not. Set a specific income target, a specific debt target, and a specific end date. Work toward all three simultaneously.

## Step Seven: Automate Payments and Stop Thinking About It Daily

Set every minimum payment to autopay so you never miss one. Set your extra payment to your target debt on the same day each month.

Then stop checking obsessively. A weekly review is enough. Daily checking of balances feeds anxiety without accelerating payoff.

The process works when you let it run consistently in the background rather than scrutinizing it daily hoping the balance drops faster than math allows.

## Step Eight: Celebrate Milestones Without Undoing Them

When a debt is paid off, mark it. Tell someone. Do something small to acknowledge the win.

Then immediately redirect that payment to the next debt rather than absorbing it back into spending. The momentum of the snowball or avalanche only works if the payment keeps rolling.

Getting out of debt takes longer than people expect and produces results that are more life-changing than they anticipated. Both of those things are true. Start anyway.
