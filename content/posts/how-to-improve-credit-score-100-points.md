A credit score is not a permanent verdict on your financial character.

It is a number calculated from specific information in your credit report, and because it is calculated, it can be changed by changing the inputs. Some inputs move the number faster than others. If you focus your energy on the right ones in the right order, a hundred-point improvement in six months is realistic for most people starting from a damaged or thin credit history.

## Understand What Actually Determines Your Score

Your FICO score, the most commonly used credit score, is calculated from five factors with very different weights.

Payment history accounts for thirty-five percent of your score. This is whether you pay on time. It is the single most important factor.

Credit utilization accounts for thirty percent. This is the percentage of your available credit that you are currently using. Using five hundred dollars on a one thousand dollar limit card is fifty percent utilization.

Length of credit history accounts for fifteen percent. Older accounts help. Closing old accounts hurts.

Credit mix accounts for ten percent. Having both revolving credit, like credit cards, and installment credit, like loans, helps slightly.

New credit inquiries account for ten percent. Applying for multiple new credit accounts in a short period hurts temporarily.

Knowing these weights tells you exactly where to focus.

## Month One: Dispute Every Error on Your Credit Report

Get your credit reports from all three bureaus at annualcreditreport.com. You are entitled to free reports from Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion.

Go through every account on every report. Look for accounts that are not yours, late payments that were actually paid on time, incorrect balances, accounts that should have fallen off after seven years, and duplicate collections.

Dispute every error directly with the bureau reporting it. Disputes are submitted online, by mail, or by phone. Bureaus have thirty days to investigate and respond.

Errors on credit reports are more common than most people realize. Removing inaccurate negative information can produce significant score increases quickly.

## Month One: Pay Down Credit Card Balances

Credit utilization is the fastest-moving factor in your credit score and the one you have the most immediate control over.

The target utilization for the best credit scores is under ten percent. Under thirty percent is the widely cited guideline, but getting to ten percent or below has a more dramatic positive impact.

If you have three credit cards with a combined limit of five thousand dollars and you currently carry balances totaling two thousand dollars, your utilization is forty percent. Paying that down to five hundred dollars drops utilization to ten percent and can produce a significant score increase within one to two billing cycles.

Pay down the card with the highest utilization percentage first, not necessarily the highest balance.

## Month Two: Set Every Account to Autopay

One missed payment can drop your score significantly. A pattern of late payments is the most damaging thing possible for your credit.

Set every account with a minimum payment to autopay for at least the minimum. You can always pay more manually. But the autopay ensures you never accidentally miss a payment because you forgot or were busy.

If you currently have accounts in collections for missed payments, paying current bills on time consistently is the most important thing you can do going forward. Recent positive payment history gradually offsets older negative history.

## Month Two: Become an Authorized User on a Responsible Account

Ask a family member or close friend with a long-standing, well-managed credit card if they will add you as an authorized user on their account.

When you are added as an authorized user, that account's history often appears on your credit report. A card with a long history, low utilization, and no late payments can give your score a significant boost within one to two months of being added.

You do not need to use the card or even have physical access to it. The benefit comes from the account history appearing on your report.

## Month Three: Address Collection Accounts

If you have accounts in collections, they are significantly harming your score.

Paying a collection does not always remove it from your credit report, but it changes the status from unpaid to paid, which some scoring models treat more favorably. Newer FICO models ignore paid collections entirely.

Before paying any collection, request debt validation in writing. The collector must prove the debt is yours and the amount is accurate before you are obligated to pay.

Negotiate. Collectors often purchase debts for a fraction of their face value and will accept less than the full amount to settle. Always get any settlement agreement in writing before sending payment.

If the collection is near the seven-year mark, it will fall off your report automatically. Check the date of first delinquency before paying.

## Month Four and Five: Do Not Open New Accounts, Do Not Close Old Ones

New credit applications generate hard inquiries that temporarily lower your score. During an active credit improvement period, avoid applying for new credit unless absolutely necessary.

Do not close old credit card accounts even if you do not use them. Closing an account reduces your available credit, which increases utilization. It also reduces your average age of accounts over time. Both effects lower your score.

Keep old accounts open with a small recurring charge and pay the balance monthly.

## Month Six: Check Your Progress and Adjust

Pull your credit reports again at the six-month mark. Compare the scores to your starting point.

You should see measurable improvement if you have been consistent with payments, reduced utilization, and disputed any errors. The degree of improvement depends on your starting score and which factors were most damaged to begin with.

People with lower starting scores often see the largest improvements because there is more room to move. Someone starting at 520 can realistically reach 620 or higher in six months with focused effort. Someone starting at 680 can realistically reach 730 or above.

The score you have today is not the score you are stuck with. It is just the score you have right now.
